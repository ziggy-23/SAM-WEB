## Overview

At the General Secretariat of the League of Arab States, I worked within the Department of the Horn of Africa and Sudan — an introduction to regional affairs far from the usual centres of international attention.

## What I did

My main focus was the Sudanese civil war and its political, military, humanitarian and regional dimensions. I followed it through international and regional media, broadcasts, reports and analysis, trying to piece together a fragmented picture of a conflict that received far less attention than its scale warranted.

I prepared reports on Sudan that looked beyond the battlefield — in particular its agricultural potential and its historical role as a breadbasket for the region, given its land, its water and its place in the Nile basin. I also explored the wider Horn of Africa: relations between neighbours, regional security, and the strategic importance of the Red Sea.

Conversations with my manager about maritime chokepoints, trade routes and regional security sparked the question that would later become my Master's thesis.

<Learned quote="Research is not simply about collecting information; it is about establishing relationships between events, identifying what is missing and recognising the limits of what can be known.">

Cairo changed the way I looked at Sudan and the Horn of Africa. Following the war closely forced me past the daily headlines towards the historical, economic and geographical conditions that made it so consequential.

Working with fragmented, contradictory and hard-to-verify sources taught me patience, comparison and a willingness to reconsider what I thought I understood.

Sudan's agricultural importance showed me how closely food security is tied to geopolitics: a war in a country with such resources is not only a domestic crisis, but a question of regional stability, migration and food production far beyond its borders.

And the Red Sea stopped being just a body of water. It became a strategic corridor between Europe, Asia and Africa — and a subject worth pursuing in depth.

Then there was Egypt itself: a city with its own rhythm, where an ancient civilisation sits alongside a vast contemporary metropolis. I left with a deeper interest in a part of the world that deserves far more attention, and the beginnings of a question that still occupies me today.
