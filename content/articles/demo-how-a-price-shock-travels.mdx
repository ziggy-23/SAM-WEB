<Lede>
When the price of oil rises, the effect does not arrive all at once. It travels — through trade balances, currencies, prices and, eventually, politics. This demonstration follows that path step by step.
</Lede>

<DemoNotice />

<Step aside={<Scribble rotate={-3}>this piece is a format test — the mechanics are generic, the numbers invented</Scribble>}>

## 01 — The starting point

Imagine a small economy that imports almost all of its energy. It pays for fuel in dollars, earns dollars mainly through exports, remittances and tourism, and keeps a buffer of foreign-currency reserves.

For a country like this, the oil price is not an abstract market figure. It is a <Mark>monthly bill</Mark>.

</Step>

<Step aside={<BarChart title="Energy import bill" unit="index, before = 100" illustrative data={[{ label: "Before the shock", value: 100 }, { label: "After a sustained price rise", value: 135, highlight: true }]} />}>

## 02 — The import bill grows

If the volume of fuel the country needs stays roughly the same, a higher price simply means a larger bill. Demand for fuel tends to respond slowly — people still need to drive, heat homes and run power plants.

So the first effect is mechanical: <Mark kind="circle">more dollars leave the country</Mark> for the same amount of energy.

</Step>

<Step aside={<Flow steps={["Higher oil price", "Larger import bill", "Wider trade deficit", "Pressure on reserves"]} />}>

## 03 — From the bill to the balance of payments

A bigger import bill widens the trade deficit. Unless exports, remittances or capital inflows rise to match it, the gap has to be financed — often by drawing down reserves or borrowing.

This is the moment where an energy story becomes a macroeconomic one.

</Step>

<Step aside={<LineChart title="Foreign-currency reserves" unit="months of import cover" illustrative data={[6, 5.8, 5.5, 5.1, 4.6, 4.2, 4.0, 3.9]} labels={["M0", "", "", "", "", "", "", "M7"]} />}>

## 04 — The buffer thins

Reserves are measured in what they can buy. As the import bill rises, each dollar of reserves covers fewer weeks of imports — even before a single dollar is spent.

<Scribble inline>watch this line, not the headline price</Scribble>

</Step>

<Step aside={<Stat value="3 channels" label="through which a currency under pressure reaches households: fuel prices, food prices and imported goods" />}>

## 05 — When the currency gives way

If markets doubt that reserves will hold, the currency can weaken. A weaker currency makes every import more expensive — not just fuel. This is how an energy shock becomes a <Mark kind="box">cost-of-living</Mark> shock.

</Step>

<Step aside={<Timeline items={[{ label: "Weeks", text: "Pump prices adjust, where they are not subsidised" }, { label: "Months", text: "Transport and food costs follow" }, { label: "Quarters", text: "Wages, budgets and subsidies come under strain" }, { label: "Years", text: "Policy and political consequences" }]} />}>

## 06 — The timing matters

Not everything moves at the same speed. Prices at the pump can adjust within weeks; wage negotiations, budgets and political consequences take far longer.

Good analysis keeps both clocks in view.

</Step>

## 07 — Geography is not neutral

Where a country sits — near producers, on shipping routes, inside currency unions or outside them — shapes how hard a shock lands. A small map in the margin can make that argument faster than a paragraph.

</Step>
