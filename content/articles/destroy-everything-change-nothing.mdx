<Lede>

It was supposed to “<Mark kind="strike">treat the whole world</Mark>”. Sold as a lightning operation that would neatly replace a hated regime with a more accommodating one, the war on Iran is now entering its eighth month, and nothing suggests it will be the last.

</Lede>

<Step aside={<PullQuote by="John Mearsheimer, on the war">The masterpiece of strategic error.</PullQuote>}>

## Judged by what it changes

On paper, the record is impressive: thousands of strikes, a Supreme Leader eliminated, a considerable share of Iran's military apparatus reduced to ashes. But a war is not judged by what it destroys. It is judged by <Mark>what it changes</Mark>. And that is precisely where Donald Trump's account becomes much harder to defend.

What happens next? Will there even *be* a “next”? Lacking an answer, a provisional conviction: never underestimate either human stupidity or human ingenuity. The news reminds us of both every morning.

The American political scientist John Mearsheimer calls this conflict “the masterpiece of strategic error”. The verdict is harsh; it is hard to dispute.

</Step>

<Step aside={<Flow title="Three miscalculations" steps={["Iran is about to get the bomb (never demonstrated)", "Air superiority alone will make the regime collapse", "Closing Hormuz won't hurt that much", "Four objectives, none fully achieved"]} />}>

## Three bets, all lost

It all began with the claim, never demonstrated, that Iran was on the verge of acquiring a nuclear weapon. Anyone who felt a sense of *déjà vu* was not mistaken: in the Middle East, history has an unfortunate habit of stuttering.

Donald Trump then believed that air superiority alone would bring the regime down. Finally, he underestimated how serious, operationally and economically, a closure of the <Mark kind="circle">Strait of Hormuz</Mark> would be.

The result: of the four objectives Washington set itself, none has been <Mark kind="box">fully achieved</Mark>. Let's go through them.

</Step>

## A Guide fallen, a regime standing

Remember Donald Trump promising the Iranian people freedom, urging them to “seize the moment” and “take back your country”. The calculation was simple: hit hard enough that the Islamic Republic collapses from within.

Militarily, Washington achieved what many thought unthinkable: Ali Khamenei was killed. The Islamic Republic, much less so. His son, Mojtaba Khamenei, succeeded him, while the Revolutionary Guards and the security apparatus tightened their grip. Far from the opening Washington hoped for, the old Guide's death has fed authoritarian continuity, even a hardening.

The first paradox of this war: the United States achieved the <Mark>decapitation</Mark> of the regime without achieving its <Mark kind="circle">overthrow</Mark>. One man fell. The system is still standing.

<Scribble inline rotate={-3}>decapitation ≠ regime change</Scribble>

</Step>

## The nuclear file: damaged, not settled

Donald Trump was categorical: “They will not be able to <Mark>enrich uranium</Mark>.” The ambition was not just to prevent an Iranian bomb; it was to substantially reduce, or even wipe out, Tehran's very capacity to enrich.

The bombing dealt a severe blow to the nuclear infrastructure. But levelling buildings is not the same as erasing decades of industrial, scientific and human know-how. Denied access to several sites, the International Atomic Energy Agency can no longer verify what really remains of the programme. That is precisely the question Washington claims to have settled.

And here is the embarrassing detail: on 6 October, with fighting still under way, Vice-President J. D. Vance was explaining that the United States demanded concrete guarantees on enrichment from Tehran before agreeing to end the conflict. In other words, if the objective had been reached, Washington <Mark kind="box">would not be negotiating</Mark> to obtain it.

The Iranian nuclear file is not “settled”. It is damaged, opaque, and still at the heart of the confrontation.

</Step>

<Step aside={<Callout title="A factory vs a capability"><p>A factory can be bombed. A capability made of engineers, stockpiles and dispersed, buried sites is far harder to remove.</p></Callout>}>

## Missiles: destroyed yesterday, rebuilt underground

On the ballistic front, the American record is more spectacular, and even more revealing of the limits of a war waged from the sky. The White House promised to destroy Iran's arsenal and the means to produce a new one, then announced that both had been practically wiped out.

Then Iran started again. According to American and regional reports in September, Tehran resumed limited production of ballistic missiles in <Mark kind="circle">underground facilities</Mark>, drawing in particular on components stockpiled before the war. The pace is no longer what it was before the conflict; the capacity to regenerate remains.

That is the whole difference. Donald Trump wanted Iran to be unable to make missiles. He got far less: a programme severely maimed, but able to <Mark>rise again underground</Mark>.

</Step>

## The “axis of resistance”: weakened, not defeated

This is probably where the record is most ambiguous. Washington wanted to stop Iran from “arming, funding and directing” the groups that extend its influence beyond its borders. And here it can claim real results: Hezbollah has been weakened, Hamas has suffered considerable losses, and the regional architecture Tehran patiently built is clearly less formidable than before the war.

But the <Mark kind="circle">Houthis</Mark> tell a different story. In Yemen they remain capable of large-scale operations and have even consolidated their positions around the Bab el-Mandeb strait. Their capacity to cause regional harm is intact.

The Iranian network is weakened, not eliminated. And the paradox goes further: Tehran seems to have learned from the conflict, now betting less on its armed allies than on its own missiles, drones and naval assets. Washington may have broken the <Mark>form</Mark> of Iranian power; it has not destroyed its <Mark kind="box">substance</Mark>.

Add to this the damage done to the world economy by the closure of Hormuz, and the wear on an American alliance system weakened in the Gulf and beyond. And that, truth be told, is only half the indictment.

</Step>

## Oil, or the surprise of resilience

So the stupidity is impressive. There is, however, a counter-argument, and a big one: the price of oil. If the war is a political failure, its economic damage has so far remained surprisingly contained.

Plenty of experts predicted <Mark kind="strike">$200 a barrel</Mark>, an inflationary spike and a global recession. Crude in the end peaked at around <Mark kind="circle">$126</Mark> in April 2026, and its inflation-adjusted price remains close to its average since 1972. How, then, can anyone call this “the greatest threat to energy security” of our time?

<Scribble inline rotate={-2}>the dog that didn't bark</Scribble>

</Step>

<Step aside={<BarChart title="Middle East crude exports" unit="million barrels per day" data={[{ label: "February 2026 (before the war)", value: 19.5 }, { label: "…of which through Hormuz", value: 9.7 }, { label: "End of September 2026", value: 16.3, highlight: true }]} source="Preliminary estimates, end of September 2026." />}>

## Four shock absorbers

There are several reasons.

1. **The energy transition** has reduced advanced economies' dependence on oil.
2. **Producers outside the Middle East** were able to raise output quickly.
3. **Supply chains** showed an ability to adapt that few anticipated.
4. **Global stocks** filled the remaining gap.

At the end of September, according to preliminary estimates, the region's crude exports stood at around 16.3 million barrels a day, against 19.5 million in February, of which nearly 9.7 million then passed through Hormuz.

Lose the strait that carried half the region's exports, and exports fall by only about <Mark kind="circle">a sixth</Mark>. That is the surprise.

</Step>

<Step aside={<Flow title="Why the cushion is thin" steps={["Stocks only buy time", "Refined products bite first: diesel, gas, sulphur", "AI investment props up growth, but only while it accelerates", "Debt leaves little room for a new shock", "A tight market could become a real crisis"]} />}>

## Resilience bought on credit

This flexibility is remarkable. It is not free. Stocks are not a source of supply: <Mark>they only buy time</Mark>.

Above all, we do not consume crude oil but refined products: diesel, petrol, kerosene. That is where the strain became visible. Demand for diesel soared to the point that the Trump administration briefly considered, before backing off, suspending American exports. Natural gas and sulphur saw far sharper rises than crude.

If the world economy absorbed the shock, it is also thanks to the wave of investment triggered by the rise of artificial intelligence. But what supports growth is not the *level* of that investment: it is its <Mark kind="box">rate of growth</Mark>. Let it plateau, and the cushion disappears. Add another fragility: an era of ever-deepening debt. France, the United Kingdom and even the United States are reaching debt levels that leave very little room to absorb a new shock.

The energy shock should remain contained. Nothing, however, guarantees it. The drawdown of stocks cannot last for ever, and China may not keep its domestic demand compressed for long. If those trends reverse, prices could climb to levels capable of causing major disruption. A tight market would then become a real crisis.

</Step>

## How does a war without a winner end?

The heaviest question remains: where does this war lead? Donald Trump might see the outcome of the midterm elections as a chance to give free rein to his bellicosity. The lame duck would then decide the time had come to “settle accounts” with Iran. But how far could Tehran, and how far would it want to, go in destroying its own oil infrastructure and its neighbours'? Conversely, could a renewed Congress, and would it want to, put a stop to the escalation?

There remains a more banal hypothesis, and perhaps a more likely one: that Donald Trump eventually <Mark kind="circle">gets bored</Mark>, letting hostilities wither into an acrimonious and probably informal ceasefire. Washington would extract a deal limiting enrichment and present it as a triumph; Tehran would consider its mere survival a victory.

One of the few advantages of capricious despots is that they tire quickly. It is hard to imagine Donald Trump still taking the slightest pleasure in this war. Even he must struggle to see it as a resounding triumph: as Mearsheimer points out, none of his objectives has been achieved. Iran is bruised, but its odious regime and its influence in the Gulf look firmly anchored. The temptation will be great to declare victory and move on, and American public opinion would not complain.

</Step>

<Step aside={<PullQuote by="T. S. Eliot, “The Hollow Men”">Not with a bang but a whimper.</PullQuote>}>

## Enough to destroy, not enough to win

For that may be Washington's real problem: a war can be destructive enough to rule out any return to the status quo, without being victorious enough to impose a new one. The United States has won enough battles not to speak of military failure, but too few to claim a political victory.

After eight months of war, the question is no longer who has won. It is more embarrassing than that: what can Donald Trump still <Mark kind="box">call a victory</Mark>? Unless it all ends, in T. S. Eliot's words, “not with a bang but a whimper”.

</Step>
