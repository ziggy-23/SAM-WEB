## Overview

My Master's at IBEI let me approach international affairs from a more interdisciplinary, economically grounded perspective. Specialising in International Political Economy, I focused on how states interact through trade, investment, financial markets, monetary arrangements and international institutions.

The programme opened up the mechanics of an interconnected global economy: commercial routes, supply chains, tariffs, trade agreements, protectionism, economic integration and shared currencies — and the political decisions behind the movement of goods and capital.

My time at the League of Arab States gave all this a concrete dimension. Sudan and the strategic importance of the Red Sea became the starting point of my thesis on the disruption of the Red Sea shipping corridor and international trade.

<Learned quote="Maps are not simply representations of territory; they can help explain power, dependency, vulnerability and opportunity.">

IBEI taught me that international relations cannot be studied apart from the economic systems through which countries interact. A tariff can reshape commercial relationships; a maritime disruption can affect supply chains thousands of kilometres away; an investment decision can carry consequences for security and foreign policy.

I developed a different appreciation of geography — the width of a strait, the location of a port, access to a sea — and of the contradictions of globalisation: the same networks that connect economies can become instruments of pressure and disruption.

It taught me to ask, of any international event: whose economic interests are involved, which institutions shape the outcome, who benefits and who bears the costs?

Barcelona, a Mediterranean port city with a long commercial history, was a fitting place to ask those questions every day.
